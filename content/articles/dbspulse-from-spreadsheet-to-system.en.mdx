Performance review often gets described as “filling out a form.” Inside an organization it
is usually a multi-stage chain: initial data entry, intermediate review, final approval, and
analysis that is supposed to inform HR decisions. On the project that became DbsPulse, that
chain lived inside a pharmaceutical company on Excel and paper forms — with no clear
traceability, and a real risk of human error in scoring and final approval.

The problem was never “make the form digital.” When a process has multiple stages and
different owners, missing traceability means you cannot answer basic questions: where did
this score come from, who changed it, and was there enough written evidence for a non-neutral
rating? Those gaps turn performance management into a pile of scattered files.

## Why the first attempt was Excel anyway

The first natural response was the tool the organization already knew: a complex Excel
workbook, this time with VBA modules so parts of the business logic could live inside the
file. That choice was rational. Excel was available, felt trainable, and could show an early
shape of the process faster than a full application.

The ceiling showed up quickly. Real scale needs concurrent users, distinct roles, change
history, access control, and durable analysis. A workbook — even a clever VBA workbook — is
still a file. Business logic stays hidden inside macros, versions multiply, and the source of
truth becomes fuzzy again. That is the moment an “internal tool” stops solving the problem
and becomes an operational problem of its own.

## The pivot to a web application

For real scalability, the architecture pivoted fully to a web application: FastAPI on the
server, PostgreSQL for data, and React for the interface. The stack was not a fashion choice.
It answered needs Excel could not meet: reliable authentication and authorization, structured
storage, and a UI that can make a multi-stage workflow explicit — from initial entry through
review, approval, and final analysis.

In that form, DbsPulse became an organizational platform for managing performance evaluation:
turning a complex manual workflow into a structured, traceable, analyzable system. A digital
form is not enough. The system has to encode business rules in the architecture, not only in
a training document.

## Three architectural decisions ahead of the UI

Three decisions marked the difference between a polished interface and a system you can trust.

**First: HR-only manual access assignment.** In sensitive HR workflows, self-signup or
automatic role inference can look faster and still create ambiguity. In DbsPulse, access is
assigned manually by HR — so the boundary of “who enters the evaluation cycle” has an
organizational owner instead of leaking through a fuzzy technical rule.

**Second: a mandatory 15-word evidence text for any non-neutral score.** A score without
evidence is just a number. When a rating leaves the neutral state, the system requires at
least fifteen words of explanation. The rule is simple and operationally sharp: it slows
down careless scoring and leaves a readable trail for later review stages.

**Third: an automatically locked “unit supervisor” toggle when the job title is exactly
“Manager.”** Some roles should not be a momentary UI choice. When the job title matches the
expected value exactly — “Manager” — the unit-supervisor switch locks automatically. Exact
title matching, not fuzzy guessing, is deliberate: the rule must be explainable and
auditable.

<Callout variant="note" title="Product versus demo">
  None of these rules are decorative. They are the gap between a nice form and an
  organizational process you can actually defend.
</Callout>

## What was delivered

The outcome was not a prototype alone. The work produced a technical architecture document, a
stakeholder sign-off document, and a complete build specification — turning a scattered
manual process into a structured, traceable system. For a team that will continue
implementation later, that delivery package matters: decisions are written down, boundaries
are explicit, and the path from spreadsheet to system is no longer a slogan.

If DbsPulse has one durable lesson, it is this: tools earn trust when they carry the
organization’s real rules — not when they only put a modern skin on the same old mess.
